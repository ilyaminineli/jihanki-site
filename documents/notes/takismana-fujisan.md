# Fujisan

I knew climbing Mount Fuji would be an unusual experience. But what surprised me most wasn’t how hard it was to get up there. It was how differently I saw the same mountain over the course of a single day.



We left the car in Fujikawaguchiko. By the time we’d found the right stop and waited for the bus, I was itching to get on the trail. The bus climbed through the forest for nearly an hour, and when we got off, the trailhead was already crowded. Then I found out they wouldn’t let me start without long pants. I had to buy a pair on the spot. The trail fee wasn’t cheap either, and by the time we finally set out, I no longer thought we’d make it to the top. In the mountains, it’s better to give yourself some leeway. We’d go as far as we could, as long as we could get back down before dark.

But Eli-san and I found a good pace. Rocky stretches gave way to bare slopes where volcanic gravel slid underfoot with every step. Some of the little stones were black, some reddish, some golden. The water in our backpacks was running out fast, and at each station it cost a little more. Near the top, the price of a bottle was already astronomical, although even up there we found our old friends, the jihanki vending machines.

On the last stretch I started to feel dizzy. I’d been up to around three thousand meters before, but never this high (4000 meters). I could see that Eli-san was tired too, though he was still in good spirits. And we made it.



It was cooler up there, and the wind was strong. We stopped at the shrine, bought goshuin slips, and walked to the edge of the crater. I wanted to go all the way around it, but I knew we wouldn’t make it down before dark if we did.

That was where I first felt that Fuji-san had a personality of her own. Until then, the day had been all stations, bottles of water, gravel underfoot, and people on the trail. At the crater, none of it seemed to matter anymore. I felt the presence of a woman of immense strength. That day, she’d been kind to us. And I realized how much I loved her.



Going down was harder than going up. We did more sliding than walking on the loose gravel, as though we were on skis or skates. We took the wrong turn at a fork and ended up on a trail leading into the neighboring prefecture. We wouldn’t be taking the same bus back. But right then, the important thing was just to get down.

Eli-san’s leg started hurting, and every step was a struggle for him. When we finally left the gravel behind, we reached a place where we could sit down, buy water, and tend to our sore, blistered feet. By then it didn’t matter what a bottle cost. We walked on through the woods. It was a little easier there, though the bus stop still felt a long way off.

It took several buses to get back to Fujikawaguchiko. On one of them, Eli-san fell asleep with his head on my shoulder. After everything that had happened that day, there was something deeply comforting about it.

It wasn’t until we got back in the car that I realized how hungry and tired we were. We drove to our McDonald’s, the one with the view of Fuji. I couldn’t tell whether to call it lunch or dinner. We sat by the window and looked at the mountain. A few hours earlier, we’d been standing at the crater in the blazing sun and wind. Now we were safely down, and we could just sit and look at Fuji-san.
