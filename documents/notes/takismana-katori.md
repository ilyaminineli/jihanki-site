# Katori

I left Katori until our last day in Japan, even though it was one of the places I’d wanted to visit from the start. I practice a Japanese martial art that takes its name from Katori, where it began, and I wanted to see the place for myself. I thought it was somewhere near Tokyo. I was sure we had time.

Eli-san and I caught an early bus out of Kawaguchiko and reached Tokyo around eleven. We needed to get to the shrine by five and be at the airport that evening. Luckily, we could leave our suitcases at a luggage counter and have them sent straight to our hotel.
It took us a while at the bus terminal to find a route that worked: a bus to Kashima, then a local train. We bought one-way bus tickets because I wasn’t sure we’d come back the same way. We had about five hundred yen in cash between us, but until then we’d managed to pay for everything by card.
Eli-san watched Tokyo’s bridges and skyscrapers give way to open fields while I put on my headphones and made up tunes on my Nintendo. In Kashima, we had time to stop at Kashima Jingū before heading to the railway station.
We’d stopped in Kashima to catch a train, but the two shrines were part of a much older journey. Kashima Jingū, Katori Jingū, and Ikisu Jinja in Kamisu form a pilgrimage triangle (https://www.tokyobus.or.jp/aroundtokyo/togokusansha-shinshoji/index_en.html) known as the Togoku Sansha, the three great shrines of eastern Japan (https://www.japan.travel/japan-heritage/popular/edo-travelogue-hokuso-chiba-sakura-narita-sawara-choshi). In the Edo period, pilgrims traveled along the Tone River by boat to visit the three, some of them on their way home from the Grand Shrine at Ise (https://togokusuigo.com/en/visiting-three-shrines-in-togoku/).
At the station, we found out that train tickets were cash only. We had enough to buy them, with a little left over for the way back.

It was about a half-hour walk from Katori Station to the shrine. Under the blazing sun, that half hour seemed to go on forever. Our bags were heavy, and there was nowhere to buy water or stop and rest. The heat made the whole thing feel slightly unreal: that morning we left our place on Saiko Lake, we’d been on a bus to Tokyo with our suitcases, and now we were walking in full sun toward a place I’d only ever seen in photographs. 
Katori Jingū moved me more than I’d expected. We didn’t get to see the dojo, and the shops were closed, so I didn’t buy a bokken, the wooden practice sword I’d been hoping for. But we dropped a few coins into the offering box, and I pasted a goshuin, a slip of paper bearing the shrine’s seal and calligraphy, into my book. We didn’t have enough left for one for Eli-san. But finally we found shade and walked the rest of the way through the woods.

Back in Kashima, there was a bus to Tokyo every half hour. The trouble was, we couldn’t buy a ticket by card, and there wasn’t enough money on our transport cards. I ran all over Kashima trying to find a way to buy that ticket. At 7-Eleven I learned that I could top up a transport card, but only with cash. We asked the driver if we could pay when we got to Tokyo. He said no.
The sun was sinking, and the last bus would leave soon. I kept picturing us stranded there without any money while our plane took off without us. We noticed a taxi. I knew it would be expensive, but we had to make that flight or risk overstaying our visas. I prayed the payment would go through. It did. Converted to rubles, the fare came to about twenty-five thousand.
By the time we got into the taxi, I were absolutely nervous. Eli-san held my hand the whole way. He fell asleep without letting go. I still couldn’t quite believe we were actually on our way to the airport.
That taxi felt like a portal into another world. Two hours earlier, I’d been afraid we’d have nowhere to sleep but a bench at a small-town railway station. Now we were in a Tokyo airport that looked like the future, with a cool, comfortable room waiting for us and our suitcases already there. We had time to eat, rest, and go up to the observation deck. For the first time all day I felt completely at ease.
We found a few coins in one of our bags and spent them on one last drink from a jihanki. I’d always thought of jihanki as respawn points. If anything happened to us on the way, I thought, it would be a good place to respawn right here, on a Tokyo airport observation deck after dark, with a jihanki beside me.
